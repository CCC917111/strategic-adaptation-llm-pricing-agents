# Related Work

This review develops the research problem from the repeated-pricing setting to the specific gap studied here. It is organized by the role each literature plays in the argument, rather than by publication date or by the order in which our experiments were implemented.

## 1. Repeated algorithmic pricing

Algorithmic pricing is a multi-agent learning problem: every firm adapts to a market that is also changing because other firms adapt. A standard testbed places two differentiated products in a repeated market with logit demand and an outside option. Firms choose prices simultaneously, observe realized outcomes, and update their behavior over time.

[Calvano et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190623) show that independent Q-learning agents can converge to prices above the one-shot Nash equilibrium without explicit communication. Their learned policies also respond to deviations in ways that resemble reward and punishment. This finding established stable supracompetitive pricing as an important outcome in repeated algorithmic markets.

That outcome is not confined to a single implementation. The broader literature studies how feedback, exploration, market structure, demand, and the number or type of agents affect convergence and price levels. [Bichler, Durmann, and Oberlechner (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12599-025-00965-z) survey this larger learning-in-games literature.

## 2. High prices and the collusion-identification problem

The central question is not only whether prices exceed a competitive benchmark, but why. [Calvano et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijindorg.2023.102973) distinguish genuine reward–punishment strategies from spurious supracompetitive outcomes generated by the learning process. [Asker, Fershtman, and Pakes (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1111/jems.12516) show that algorithm design and counterfactual feedback can materially change pricing outcomes. [Banchio and Mantegazza](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05946) identify statistical coupling between learners as another route to elevated prices, while [Epivent and Lambin (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econlet.2024.111661) show that punishment-looking responses are not conclusive because similar patterns may follow upward deviations.

These studies imply a stricter interpretation standard. A stable high price is an empirical pattern to explain. Evidence for collusion also requires tests of the underlying policy, such as controlled deviations, off-path responses, counterfactual behavior, and robustness to changes in the learning protocol.

## 3. LLM pricing agents: actions, language, and memory

LLM pricing agents differ from tabular reinforcement learners because they enter the market with a pretrained language policy and adapt at test time through instructions, context, interaction history, and natural-language memory. [Fish, Gonczarowski, and Shorrer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806) show that LLM pricing agents can reach supracompetitive outcomes in short repeated interactions. Their prompt variations and off-path tests also demonstrate that the prompt and response scaffold are part of the experimental environment. [Luo, Schoepflin, and Wang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17203) formalize the deployment-time problem by separating a pretrained initial policy from the adaptation that occurs during a finite game.

LLM experiments can also record several information channels at once. An agent may choose a price, provide a public justification, report whether it used competitor information, and write private notes that persist to the next round. *Oversight Is Not Compliance* (public citation metadata TBD) studies these channels under passive, revision, and veto oversight. Its importance for the present project is the distinction between observable compliance and the pricing policy: a regulator can alter an executed action, and an agent can produce acceptable public language, without demonstrating that the agent's underlying strategy has changed. Public explanations and self-disclosures may also diverge from behavior or private notes.

Natural-language traces are therefore useful evidence, but not direct access to the model's internal policy. They should be compared with executed prices and tested through interventions rather than interpreted on their own.

## 4. Robustness and nonstationary environments

Recent work tests whether LLM-agent outcomes survive changes in information, patience, market structure, and interaction partners. [Keppo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20281) find that additional competitors, unequal information, different levels of patience, and interaction with other algorithm types can weaken coordination; some forms of model heterogeneity instead produce leader–follower behavior. [Agrawal et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01413) report sensitivity to model choice, communication, oversight, and environmental pressure in continuous double auctions.

Nonstationary demand is already important in reinforcement-learning pricing. [Algorithmic Collusion under Observed Demand Shocks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15084), for example, studies how learned pricing changes across observed shocks. For LLM agents, however, a further question arises: an agent may reach a stable rule after very few observations, preserve that rule in natural-language notes, and then interpret non-declining profit as evidence that no change is needed. Comparing separate stationary runs does not show whether such an agent can detect and respond to gradual change during one deployment.

## 5. Gap addressed by this project

The current literature leaves three connected uncertainties:

1. rapid convergence can indicate a strategic equilibrium or simply insufficient exploration;
2. public explanations, self-reports, private notes, and actions need not be faithful to one another; and
3. adaptation within a gradually changing LLM-agent market is less understood than outcomes in fixed environments.

This project studies the third issue while measuring the first two. In a controlled two-agent market, it compares constant demand with gradual common demand growth. Agents receive qualitative market context and realized outcomes but not the numerical demand path. The main test is whether an agent revises a stabilized pricing rule as demand changes, evaluated against round-specific competitive and joint-profit benchmarks.

The completed experiments show strong behavioral inertia and scaffold sensitivity. They do not yet identify a collusive mechanism or establish a causal role for private notes. Those are limits on the current evidence, not conclusions to be filled in with textual interpretation.
