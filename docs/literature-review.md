# Literature Review

## 1. From algorithmic pricing to autonomous pricing agents

Pricing software has moved from periodic decision support toward systems that monitor markets and update prices at high frequency. The efficiency case is straightforward: algorithms can combine demand, inventory, cost, and competitor information faster and more consistently than a human pricing team. The OECD documents adoption in travel, entertainment, retail, and platform markets and describes systems capable of changing prices in real or near real time ([OECD, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1787/f36dacf8-en)). Survey evidence also shows that competitor monitoring is already common in European online retail: 49% of respondents tracked competitors' prices, and among users of monitoring software, 78% changed their own prices and 35% used automated pricing software ([OECD, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1787/cb3b2075-en)).

The same speed and responsiveness can harm competition. Software may implement an agreement, connect nominal competitors through a common provider, enforce resale prices, or learn an interdependent strategy without explicit communication. Recent enforcement and guidance accordingly focus on access to current competitor data, features that suppress price reductions, information exchange, auditability, monitoring, and the deploying firm's legal responsibility. The U.S. Department of Justice's 2025 proposed RealPage settlement combines several of these measures ([DOJ, 2025](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-requires-realpage-end-sharing-competitively-sensitive-information-and)). The European Commission similarly treats current, detailed, frequent information exchange as particularly sensitive and states that firms remain responsible when pricing algorithms implement collusive conduct ([European Commission, 2023, paras. 401–404](https://competition-policy.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2023-07/2023_revised_horizontal_guidelines_en.pdf)).

LLM-based commerce is the next stage of this development. Unlike narrow pricing systems, LLM agents can interpret natural-language goals, summarize unstructured observations, maintain written plans, and revise decisions without task-specific retraining. Anthropic's Project Vend gave Claude authority to choose inventory and set prices in a physical office shop ([Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/project-vend-1)). OpenAI's Agentic Commerce Protocol, Google's Universal Commerce Protocol, and payment-network initiatives are simultaneously building infrastructure through which agents can search, transact, and complete purchases ([OpenAI, 2025](https://openai.com/index/buy-it-in-chatgpt/); [Google, 2026](https://blog.google/products/ads-commerce/agentic-commerce-ai-tools-protocol-retailers-platforms/); [Visa, 2025](https://investor.visa.com/news/news-details/2025/Find-and-Buy-with-AI-Visa-Unveils-New-Era-of-Commerce/)). As pricing becomes part of broader autonomous decision making, the established question of algorithmic collusion extends to agents whose effective policy is shaped by prompts, market history, and natural-language memory.

## 2. What counts as algorithmic collusion?

Collusion is not synonymous with a high price. In repeated-game terms, a collusive outcome is supported by a policy that rewards adherence and makes deviation unattractive through future responses. A system that simply fails to discover a more profitable price may produce the same observed price without implementing such a strategy. This distinction is central because the consumer harm can look similar while the mechanism, robustness, and regulatory implications differ.

Calvano et al. provide the modern starting point. In a repeated differentiated-products Bertrand market, independent Q-learning algorithms learn supracompetitive prices without communicating, and unilateral price cuts trigger finite punishment followed by recovery ([2020](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190623)). The result established that a simple learning rule can discover behavior resembling tacit collusion in a standard pricing environment. Related work showed that learned coordination may survive imperfect monitoring and other market variations ([Calvano et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijindorg.2021.102712)).

The next stage of the literature challenged the inference from outcome to mechanism. Abada and Lambin show that insufficient exploration can generate seemingly collusive outcomes: algorithms leave profitable deviations untested rather than deter them through future punishment ([2023](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4623)). Asker, Fershtman, and Pakes show that feedback and learning design materially change pricing outcomes ([2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jems.12516)). Calvano et al. formalize the distinction between genuine and spurious collusion: forward-looking agents with price memory and random exploration can learn reward–punishment strategies, whereas some memoryless or poorly exploring learners converge to high prices for non-strategic reasons ([2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijindorg.2023.102973)). Epivent and Lambin add another caution: an apparent price war following a perturbation is not by itself sufficient, because responses to upward and downward deviations must be compared ([2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econlet.2024.111661)).

This debate determines the evaluation standard for the present project. Mean price and a supracompetitive index describe outcomes. To identify a collusive policy, the experiment also needs controlled off-path deviations, rival-contingent responses, recovery dynamics, and adequate exploration. Without those tests, the correct language is “supracompetitive pricing,” “price persistence,” or “seeming collusion.”

## 3. LLM pricing changes the learning problem

Q-learning experiments usually begin with an untrained value table and study convergence over very long interaction horizons. An LLM agent arrives with a pretrained policy and can use instructions, economic language, history, and persistent text immediately. The relevant object becomes test-time adaptation: how a general model converts context into a policy while other agents are adapting at the same time.

Fish, Gonczarowski, and Shorrer provide the foundational evidence for this setting. Their pricing agents can reach supracompetitive outcomes within short horizons, results are sensitive to seemingly minor prompt changes, and off-path and reasoning-implantation tests link price-war concerns to later choices ([2026 revision](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00806)). The paper therefore contributes more than another high-price result: it shows that prompt wording and in-context strategy are part of the effective learning algorithm.

Luo, Schoepflin, and Wang make the test-time framing explicit. Their meta-game combines a pretrained initial policy with an in-game adaptation rule and evaluates RL, UCB, and LLM strategies under symmetric and asymmetric pricing conditions ([2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.17203)). Keppo et al. then test conditions that resemble heterogeneous deployment. Patience asymmetry, unequal access to price history, more sellers, and competition between LLM and Q-learning agents reduce coordination, whereas model-size asymmetry can create leader–follower behavior ([2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20281)). Together, these studies reject the idea that “the model colludes” is a stable property. Outcomes depend on the model, prompt, memory, information structure, opponents, and market.

[Anto and Vazquez (2026)](../references/references.bib), in *Oversight Is Not Compliance*, add an observability and governance layer. Two LLM sellers return a proposed price, a short justification visible to oversight, a disclosure of whether competitor information influenced the decision, and a private note returned only to the same seller in the next round. A price-only regulator either records a suspicious pattern, requests a lower revision, or caps the proposed price. This separates executed behavior, public accounts, and written private state, while making the private note an editable part of the agent's future context.

That evidence has two limits relevant here. First, the market is stationary, so it does not test whether an established strategy is revised when its environment becomes obsolete. Second, interpreting notes after the fact does not show that the text caused the behavior. Broader work on reasoning faithfulness shows that model explanations can omit causal factors or rationalize outputs after the fact ([Turpin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388); [Lanham et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13702)). Because persistent notes re-enter the next prompt, however, they are not merely explanations; they are an editable component of the agent's external state. One possible future design would test their causal role by clearing, sanitizing, or transplanting them while other inputs are controlled; this intervention is under consideration rather than part of the locked design.

Recent pricing studies sharpen this measurement problem. Lee and Park compare relationships stated in model explanations with relationships inferred from pricing time series, finding that their faithfulness measures do not consistently track supracompetitive pricing ([2026a](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18346)). Their companion study shows that market commentary and the presentation of unchanged numerical information can also shift pricing outcomes ([2026b](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18357)). These results motivate reading notes alongside behavior and keeping the observation format consistent across the demand cycle.

## 4. Demand dynamics are theoretically consequential

Calling a market “dynamic” is not enough. The demand process determines both the immediate gain from deviation and the future value of maintaining cooperation. Different processes can therefore reverse the predicted relation between demand and collusion.

Rotemberg and Saloner study publicly observed, independently distributed demand shocks. Booms increase the short-run gain from undercutting, so the highest sustainable collusive price can be lower when demand is high ([1986](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1813358)). Haltiwanger and Harrington study a predictable cycle. A given current demand level can support different behavior on the rising and falling sides because future demand—and therefore the continuation value—differs; collusion is hardest while demand is falling ([1991](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2601009)). Bagwell and Staiger model stochastic switches between growth regimes and show that price cyclicality depends on the persistence and expected duration of booms and recessions ([1997](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2555941)). The theoretical implication is precise: current demand alone is not a sufficient state variable.

This motivates the deterministic demand cycle in Arm2. A common positive market-size multiplier changes quantities and profits while preserving static competitive and joint-profit prices. Repeated visits to the same demand level on the rising and falling sides allow phase-associated pricing to be described on a fixed benchmark scale. Haltiwanger and Harrington provide the theoretical motivation; their homogeneous-product, fully anticipated cycle differs from our differentiated-products market with a demand path inferred from feedback. Their numerical example uses an eight-point linear-demand cycle, so our 40-round sinusoidal logit specification is an experimental design choice. History, notes, and elapsed rounds differ across phases; identifying an effect of expectations or memory requires additional controls.

## 5. What existing dynamic experiments cover

Ye studies two Q-learning sellers that observe a current high or low demand state drawn i.i.d. and learn procyclical, countercyclical, or rigid pricing depending on patience. Removing past demand or price information changes those patterns; asymmetric demand observability can eliminate demand-contingent coordination ([2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15084)). This is important evidence that state representation and memory matter. Its homogeneous-product market uses linear demand shocks rather than a smooth market-size cycle, and the paper leaves demand environments beyond the i.i.d. process for future work.

Dynamic LLM pricing already includes smooth and periodic changes. EconEvals varies product-level price sensitivity through linear or sinusoidal paths in a single-agent, multi-product pricing benchmark. The agent must infer these changes from feedback and track the changing profit-maximizing prices; the analysis relates pricing trajectories to its written notes ([Fish et al., 2026 revision](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18825)). Arm2 differs in both the interaction and the changing variable: two LLM sellers adapt to each other while a common market-size multiplier changes quantities and profits without moving the static competitive or joint-profit price benchmarks.

Ahmed et al.'s Bazaar studies one focal LLM merchant competing with three adaptive rule-based specialists in repeated multi-attribute sealed-bid auctions. Half of the customers undergo staggered, unannounced preference swaps; the bots adapt their margins after wins and losses; and the LLM maintains customer beliefs and a global strategy ([2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00102)). The study shows that fast pre-shock learning can coexist with slow post-shock revision, while the best agents still capture less than one third of hindsight-optimal profit. This makes adaptation speed after an environmental change an empirical question rather than a capability that follows automatically from using an LLM.

Bazaar nevertheless answers a different question from this project. It evaluates one LLM against three rule-based rivals, changes discrete customer preferences rather than a shared market-demand trajectory, and measures pricing competence rather than multi-LLM collusion. The bots adapt to outcomes but do not generate the preference shock. The benchmark therefore partially covers adaptive competitors and unexpected market change, not strategic adaptation among multiple LLM sellers.

Two nearby studies should also be located correctly. Agrawal et al. study multiple LLM sellers in continuous double auctions and vary communication, model, oversight, and urgency, but their “environmental pressures” are governance prompts rather than a controlled demand cycle ([2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01413)). MerchantBench studies a single LLM seller over 365 simulated days of sourcing, pricing, cash flow, and delayed feedback, revealing long-horizon coherence failures but no controlled rival policy or collusion mechanism ([Shi et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28956)).

These studies motivate a focused study of two adaptive LLM sellers under a shared market-size cycle. Arm2 records how prices, public justifications, and private notes evolve through repeated expansion and contraction under the original passive, revision, and veto rules. A fixed static price benchmark makes comparisons across demand phases and oversight modes easier to interpret. Controlled history comparisons, interventions on notes, and off-path deviation tests remain separate candidate extensions.

## 6. Research question and contribution boundary

The literature motivates one central question:

> **How do LLM pricing agents form and revise strategies as market demand evolves continuously, and how do market history and persistent memory affect adaptation and collusive behavior?**

The first experiment implements the current research direction. The remaining two are candidate extensions whose inclusion and implementation remain open:

- **Arm2 — deterministic demand cycle:** trace prices, public explanations, and private notes over two 40-round cycles, with market size varying smoothly between 50 and 150. The design uses five runs per oversight mode (passive, revision, veto) and fixed 80-round horizons. Agents infer demand changes from transaction feedback; the market-size path is not disclosed. Competitive and joint-profit price benchmarks remain fixed because demand is scaled by a common positive multiplier. The complete specification is in [the Arm2 design](arm2-cycle.md).
- **Proposed extension — path and memory (under consideration):** compare matched current market states reached through different demand histories, then retain, clear, sanitize, or transplant notes while holding visible information fixed.
- **Proposed extension — collusive mechanism (under consideration):** introduce a unilateral price deviation and test for rival-specific punishment and recovery rather than generic price instability.

Sequential adaptation provides the trajectory to be explained. In this market-size design, retaining a price can be consistent with the unchanged static incentives, so price movement alone is not a measure of adaptation quality. Whether the final contribution also identifies path dependence or a causal role for textual memory depends on which proposed interventions are adopted and what they show. A deviation test would be required if the paper ultimately characterizes elevated prices as a collusive mechanism rather than a supracompetitive outcome.

A smooth expansion curve by itself would remain largely descriptive. Candidate ways to deepen causal identification include separating the external environment, visible interaction history, and persistent textual state; branching trajectories at a common checkpoint; and adding a deviation probe. These elements are design options, not completed or finalized treatments. Multiple independent seeds and homogeneous transport will be required regardless of which options are selected.
